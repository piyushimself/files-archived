What Is Blackout Poetry?

You take a page from a newspaper. You read it not to understand the news, but to find a secret poem hiding inside it. Then you black out every word that isn't part of your poem — and what remains is art.

This is the art Austin Kleon calls Newspaper Blackout. He took a marker to newspaper columns and found that beneath every mundane article, a stranger, truer thing was waiting. The newspaper gives you the words. You give them new meaning.

"I use other people's words to say what I have to say."
— Austin Kleon

The Three Gestures

Every blackout poem involves exactly three things:

The Fundamental Practice

  • Read — Scan the text loosely. Don't try to understand it as news. Let your eye drift to unusual words, beautiful words, unexpected phrases.
  • Circle — Mark the words you want to keep. They don't have to be consecutive. They just need to make emotional or poetic sense together.
  • Black out — Everything else disappears under your marker. What remains is your poem.
· · · · ·

A Simple Example

Here is a real newspaper sentence, and how a blackout poem can emerge from it:

City council approves new budget plan

The council voted Tuesday to approve a new fiscal plan that will affect nearly all residents, pending final approval from the state department of finance. Officials say the changes are necessary to maintain balance. The proposal was met with quiet approval.

The poem found within: Tuesday —
new plan,
residents seek balance.
Quiet approval.
✦

Why This Art Form?

Blackout poetry removes the pressure to invent from nothing. The words already exist. Your only job is to discover what's already there. This makes it perfect for moments of stillness — a commute, a lunch break, a quiet ten minutes with a stylus.

It is also, secretly, a practice in attention. The newspaper becomes a meditation object. You learn to see language as material, not just meaning.

"Every newspaper is a poem waiting to be written,
a painting waiting to be painted."
The Core Philosophy of Blackout Poetry

Before You Begin

How to Read a Page for Poetry

The single biggest mistake beginners make is reading the article. Don't. Reading for meaning activates the logical mind. You want the dreaming mind.

The Four Reading Passes

Pass 1 · The Drift

Let your eyes move freely across the page. Don't follow sentences. Notice which words call to you — visually, emotionally, phonetically. Words that feel heavy. Words that feel strange.

Pass 2 · The Circle

Lightly circle or underline words that caught your eye. Do not think about whether they connect. Just collect. Aim for 8–15 words from a paragraph.

Pass 3 · The Bridge

Look at your collected words. Which ones want to be in the same poem? Is there a relationship — an emotion, an image, a tension? This is the editorial pass. Narrow your selection to 4–8 words that feel like they belong together.

Pass 4 · The Black

Everything else goes dark. You don't have to be perfect. The blacking out is part of the artwork. The physical act of erasure is the poem's other half.

· · ·

What Makes a Good Blackout Poem?

The Unwritten Rules

  • Words must stay in their original order from left to right, top to bottom
  • You cannot add new words — only subtract
  • The remaining words should create an image, emotion, or idea that the article never intended
  • Tension between the poem's meaning and the article's meaning is a bonus, not a problem
  • Short is almost always better. 3–8 words often makes a stronger poem than 20
The best blackout poems feel like something that was always there,
waiting to be uncovered.

What to Look For

Train your eye to find these word types in any newspaper text:

verbs of motion colour words body words time words nature words silence / quiet unexpected adjectives names of places

These word-types carry emotional weight even when removed from context. A verb like vanish or a noun like dusk does its own work even alone.

What You're Learning

Notice how the meaning shifts. The article was about train delays — frustration, infrastructure, bureaucracy. The poem that emerges from it is something entirely different. The blacking-out is an act of translation.

Beginner Insight

  • The poem doesn't have to relate to the article's subject at all
  • In fact, the best poems often contradict the article's emotional register
  • An article about war can yield a poem about tenderness
  • An article about economics can yield a poem about rain

Why Intention Matters

Setting an intention before reading is a discipline borrowed from meditation. You are not passively receiving the text — you are actively hunting. This creates poems with a stronger internal coherence, because every word was chosen in service of a single feeling.

Example intentional poem: a long corridor —
hours pass,
names unspoken.
Everyone waiting
for something.
Notice how the poem contradicts the clinical tone of the article.

Level 4 · Advanced

Advanced Techniques

The basics are mastered. Now you learn to push the form — to make poems that are more surprising, more resonant, more complex.

1 · The Negative Space Poem

Instead of thinking about which words to keep, think about the shape of the blacking out. The black areas can form images — a tree, a figure, a window. The white words float inside a black landscape. This is the visual art dimension of blackout poetry.

visual poetry shape as meaning

2 · The Contradiction Poem

Deliberately find words that contradict the article's emotional tone. A war report yields a love poem. An obituary yields a birth poem. A financial report yields a nature poem. The tension between article and poem creates irony without you having to write a single word of irony.

irony juxtaposition

3 · The Found Title

Don't black out the headline. Use it, or part of it, as the title of your poem. This creates a second layer of meaning — the poem exists in dialogue with the headline above it.

dialogue layered meaning

4 · The Serial Poem

Work the same article three times with three different intentions. You will get three entirely different poems. This teaches you that the poem is not in the text — it is in the relationship between the text and your attention.

variation subjectivity

5 · The One-Word Poem

Find the single word in the entire article that carries the most weight when surrounded by complete blackout. A single word floating in a sea of black can be the most devastating poem. Try it — it's harder than it sounds.

minimalism weight of single words

For Mobile & Stylus Users

The Stylus Practice

The physical act of blacking out — the hand moving across the page — is part of what makes this art form meditative. On mobile with a stylus, you can recreate this feeling precisely.

Recommended Workflow on Mobile

The Three-App Method

  • Source: Screenshot a newspaper page, or use a newspaper PDF app. Mumbai Mirror, The Hindu, Times of India — any broadsheet works.
  • Markup: Open the screenshot in your phone's native markup tool or an app like Noteshelf, GoodNotes, or even Samsung Notes. Use a thick black brush.
  • Preserve: Save the finished image. The poem lives in the artifact — the physical mark.

Stylus Settings That Work

Brush Type

Use a flat/chisel brush rather than a round pen. The flat brush mimics a real marker and creates the characteristic newspaper-blackout texture — thick horizontal strokes that feel like real ink on newsprint.

Colour

Pure black (#000000) is the classic. But try very dark navy (#0a0a2a) or dark brown-black (#1a1008) for a slightly warmer look that photographs better. Austin Kleon uses a black Sharpie — go for full opacity.

Size

Match your brush size to roughly 1.2× the line height of the newspaper text. This lets you black out an entire line in a single pass without accidentally covering the lines above and below.

Pressure Sensitivity

Turn it off for blacking out. You want consistent coverage. Save pressure sensitivity for the circling/underlining phase where you're just marking words lightly before committing.

· · ·

The Daily Practice

Austin Kleon's original practice was simple: every day, one newspaper page. Not a poem a day — just one attempt. Most days nothing remarkable happens. But the practice builds a muscle for seeing language as material.

Five minutes a day.
One page.
No pressure for it to be good.

Where to Find Text

Sources for Mumbai-Based Practice

  • The Hindu e-paper (PDF) — excellent long-form writing with poetic vocabulary
  • Mumbai Mirror or Mid-Day — shorter, punchy sentences ideal for beginners
  • Old issues from Archive.org — historical newspapers give unusual, dated language
  • English translations of Marathi newspapers — the slightly formal translation voice creates interesting found language
  • Annual reports and corporate press releases — deliberately dull, which makes discovered poems funnier and stranger

Taking It Further

If you begin sharing your work: don't caption the poem with the article's subject. Let the poem stand alone. The viewer doesn't need to know it came from a budget report or a cricket score. The origin is yours to keep.
