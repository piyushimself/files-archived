Cinema's Erotic Masters The 15 directors profiled in this volume have each, in their own way, refused the convention that sexuality is what cinema should avoid, minimise, or handle with euphemism. They come from nine countries across seven decades. Several have won the Palme d'Or at Cannes; several have been banned, prosecuted, or censored. All of them have argued — through their work — that the body is one of cinema's essential subjects. They are not a unified school. Breillat's clinical feminist rigour has nothing in common with von Trier's operatic provocation; Imamura's earthy comedy is as far from Cronenberg's cold body horror as Tokyo from Toronto. What they share is the conviction that desire, intimacy, and sexuality are not supplements to serious filmmaking but its very substance — the territory where power, identity, history, and the human condition are most nakedly visible. Each profile includes a critical biography and a filmography of essential works. These are not recommendations for an evening's entertainment. They are invitations into a body of work. Catherine Breillat

b. 1948, Bressuire, France · Female desire from the inside · Explicit female subjectivity · Anti-sentimentality No filmmaker has pursued the question of female desire with more rigour and less sentimentality than Catherine Breillat. Beginning with Une Vraie Jeune Fille (1976) — banned for 25 years — and continuing through Romance (1999), Fat Girl (2001), and Anatomy of Hell (2004), Breillat has constructed a body of work that treats female sexuality as philosophy. Her films are often uncomfortable, frequently explicit, and always insistent that the interior life of a desiring woman has never been adequately filmed by anyone else. She has said her films are not about sex but about what sex means to the person experiencing it from inside — a completely different subject. Her 2023 film Last Summer proved her formal intelligence has not diminished.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• Une Vraie Jeune Fille (1976) — debut banned for 25 years • Romance (1999) — NC-17; female desire as manifesto • A Ma Soeur / Fat Girl (2001) — the most disturbing ending in French cinema • Sex Is Comedy (2002) — meta-examination of filming erotic scenes • Anatomy of Hell (2004) — the female body as text • The Last Mistress (2007) — period passion with Breillat's clinical eye • Last Summer (2023) — transgression in bourgeois comfort

Nagisa Oshima

b. 1932–2013, Kyoto, Japan · Sexuality as political resistance · Post-war Japanese trauma · Radical form Nagisa Oshima is the great political filmmaker of erotic cinema. His 1976 masterpiece Ai No Corrida (In the Realm of the Senses) used unsimulated sex not for titillation but as an argument: that erotic obsession is the one freedom the Japanese militarist state cannot fully colonise. Oshima saw the body as the last political territory. His work across the 1960s Japanese New Wave — from Cruel Story of Youth (1960) through Death by Hanging (1968) — established him as Japan's most formally and politically aggressive filmmaker. Ai No Corrida was shot in France because it could not be developed in Japan, and remains one of the handful of explicitly sexual films that genuinely belongs in any serious discussion of world cinema.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• Cruel Story of Youth (1960) — Japanese New Wave debut • Death by Hanging (1968) — Brechtian capital punishment • Boy (1969) — child exploitation with political fury • Ai No Corrida / In the Realm of the Senses (1976) — the definitive film • Empire of Passion (1978) — ghost story / erotic obsession • Merry Christmas Mr. Lawrence (1983) — Bowie, Sakamoto, homoerotic desire Erotic Art Cinema: Bold, Intimate & Thought-Provoking | Page 3

Bernardo Bertolucci

b. 1941–2018, Parma, Italy · Eros and politics · Italian communist desire · Beautiful formalism Bertolucci brought a Marxist's eye and a sensualist's camera to the intersection of sex and power. Last Tango in Paris (1972) remains the great film of grief and anonymous desire. The Conformist (1970) is the most visually gorgeous film ever made about fascism's relationship to repressed homosexuality. The Dreamers (2003) placed its erotic triangle inside the May 1968 Paris uprising. Bertolucci understood that political history and erotic history are the same history — the body is always the site where ideology is either enforced or resisted. His final films showed a director who never stopped believing that cinema and desire are both forms of revolution.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• The Conformist (1970) — fascism and repressed desire, Storaro's greatest cinematography • Last Tango in Paris (1972) — Brando, grief, and anonymous intimacy • 1900 (Novecento) (1976) — five-hour Marxist epic of Italian history • La Luna (1979) — incest, opera, and the bonds between mothers and sons • The Sheltering Sky (1990) — desire in the Sahara, Bowles adaptation • The Dreamers (2003) — Paris 1968, cinema, and erotic games Lars von Trier b. 1956, Copenhagen, Denmark · Female suffering as cinema · Radical form · Sexuality as theology Lars von Trier is the most divisive filmmaker working in serious cinema, and his treatment of sexuality is inseparable from his larger project of using cinema as an instrument of spiritual and psychological extremity. Breaking the Waves (1996) established his method: a woman's sexual sacrifice as religious act. Dancer in the Dark (2000), Dogville (2003), and Melancholia (2011) continued his examination of female endurance. But Nymphomaniac (2013) is his most explicit engagement with sexuality as both subject and formal strategy — a four-hour philosophical novel in which the central character's erotic life is the lens through which every question of morality, art, and meaning is examined.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• Breaking the Waves (1996) — sex as religious martyrdom • Dancer in the Dark (2000) — musical tragedy, Bjork • Dogville (2003) — bare-stage parable of exploitation • Antichrist (2009) — grief, sex, and chaos • Melancholia (2011) — depression as clear sight • Nymphomaniac Vol. I & II (2013) — the great explicit art film • The House That Jack Built (2018) — violence as aesthetic practice Erotic Art Cinema: Bold, Intimate & Thought-Provoking | Page 4

Gaspar Noe

b. 1963, Buenos Aires (works in France) · Sensory assault · Explicit sexuality · Reverse chronology · Trauma Gaspar Noe makes films designed to be physically experienced. His use of explicit sexuality is inseparable from his larger project of putting the audience inside an experience — whether that experience is the nine-minute rape sequence in Irreversible (2002) or the tender 3D lovemaking of Love (2015). Noe's controversial reputation has often obscured his genuine formal innovation: no director has used the camera's ability to disorient, seduce, and assault the viewer with more consistency. Enter the Void (2009) is a first-person journey through a Tokyo drug trip and death. Climax (2018) is a dance film that becomes a descent into drugged collective madness. He remains cinema's most extreme sensualist.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• Seul Contre Tous / I Stand Alone (1998) — debut of furious violence • Irreversible (2002) — reverse chronology, the most confrontational rape scene in cinema • Enter the Void (2009) — first-person death and reincarnation in Tokyo • Love (2015) — explicit 3D love story as elegy • Climax (2018) — dance and collective dissolution • Vortex (2021) — dual-screen dementia as love story

Paul Verhoeven

b. 1938, Amsterdam, Netherlands · Genre subversion · Sexuality as social critique · Hollywood inside-out Paul Verhoeven is the great satirist of Hollywood sexuality. His Dutch films — Turkish Delight (1973), Soldier of Orange (1977), Spetters (1980) — established a director unafraid of the body in all its states. In Hollywood, Basic Instinct (1992) used erotic thriller conventions to destabilise the male gaze entirely — Sharon Stone and Joe Eszterhas created a sensation, but Verhoeven's real subject was the camera's complicity. Showgirls (1995) is now correctly understood as the most savage satire of American ambition ever made in the guise of exploitation cinema. Elle (2016) is his masterpiece: Isabelle Huppert in a film that refuses every available narrative about rape and desire.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• Turkish Delight (1973) — erotic debut, most-seen Dutch film ever • Soldier of Orange (1977) — WWII heroism and sexuality • Spetters (1980) — Dutch working class and desire • Basic Instinct (1992) — erotic thriller as surveillance critique • Showgirls (1995) — exploitation as satire (correctly understood) • Black Book (2006) — WWII resistance and erotic survival • Elle (2016) — the definitive film about female desire and power Erotic Art Cinema: Bold, Intimate & Thought-Provoking | Page 5

Pier Paolo Pasolini

b. 1922–1975, Bologna, Italy · Sexuality as theology and politics · Italian underclass · Martyrdom Pasolini was murdered in 1975 on the eve of Salo's release, and his death reads like the logical end of a life spent making art that enraged the powerful. As a gay Marxist Catholic, Pasolini found in sexuality the nexus of every contradiction he lived: the church, the state, the body, and power were all the same battleground. His Trilogy of Life — The Decameron (1971), Canterbury Tales (1972), Arabian Nights (1974) — celebrated sexuality as the domain of the poor and the subversive. Salo (1975) used the Marquis de Sade's structure to argue that fascism is the systematic sexualisation of power. No filmmaker has treated the body with more political and theological seriousness.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• Accattone (1961) — debut, Roman underclass as sacred subject • The Gospel According to St. Matthew (1964) — Jesus as revolutionary • Teorema (1968) — a stranger's body destroys a bourgeois family • Porcile / Pigsty (1969) — cannibalism and capital • The Decameron (1971) — sexual joy as working-class freedom • Arabian Nights (1974) — erotic tales of the non-Western world • Salo, or the 120 Days of Sodom (1975) — the unsurpassable final statement

Claire Denis

b. 1946, Paris, France · The body in space · Colonialism and desire · Tactile cinema Claire Denis makes films about bodies — how they occupy space, how they touch and fail to touch, how they carry history. Her cinema is tactile: you feel the heat of Djibouti in Beau Travail (1999), the skin contact in Trouble Every Day (2001), the gravity of two people in bed in 35 Shots of Rum (2008). Denis came up through the French New Wave as assistant director to Wenders, Rivette, and Tarkovsky — and synthesised their lessons into something entirely her own. Trouble Every Day is the most extreme of her films — a horror film about desire as literal consumption. But all her films share the conviction that the cinema's job is to put you inside a body, not above it.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• Chocolat (1988) — colonial Africa and the body's knowledge • Beau Travail (1999) — Legionnaires, Djibouti, the male body as choreography • Trouble Every Day (2001) — desire as cannibalism • 35 Shots of Rum (2008) — father/daughter and the warmth of ordinary life • White Material (2009) — Isabelle Huppert in collapsing colonial Africa • Beau Travail remake / Both Sides of the Blade (2022) Erotic Art Cinema: Bold, Intimate & Thought-Provoking | Page 6

Michael Haneke

b. 1942, Munich, Germany (works in Austria/France) · Violence of bourgeois comfort · The camera as moral weapon · No catharsis Haneke withholds. His cinema of refusal — no catharsis, no comfort, no resolution — makes sexuality and violence the same category of act: something done by bodies to other bodies, in which the viewer is always implicated. The Piano Teacher (2001) is his erotic masterpiece: a repressed Viennese woman's inner life of voyeurism and masochism, played by Isabelle Huppert in a performance of absolute, terrifying honesty. Funny Games (1997 and 2007) implicates the audience in the violence they are watching. Cache / Hidden (2005) makes a colonial past the source of sexual and psychological guilt. Haneke is cinema's great moralist — and the most rigorous.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• Funny Games (1997) — audience complicity in screen violence • The Piano Teacher / La Pianiste (2001) — repression and masochism • Cache / Hidden (2005) — colonial guilt as psychological invasion • The White Ribbon (2009) — Palme d'Or; fascism's origins in puritanism • Amour (2012) — the body's decline as the final love story • Happy End (2017) — bourgeois horror in Calais

Ang Lee

b. 1954, Pingtung, Taiwan · Repression made visible · Cross-cultural desire · Genre as emotional container Ang Lee is cinema's great poet of repression. His entire career is a study in desire that cannot be expressed openly — and the various cultural containers (the Western, the martial arts film, the period romance, the NC-17 drama) that he uses to hold it. Brokeback Mountain (2005) made the Western landscape the site of forty years of suppressed love. Lust, Caution (2007) used WWII Shanghai and explicit NC-17 sex scenes to argue that intimacy is the ultimate political vulnerability. The Wedding Banquet (1993) found comedy and tragedy in the same closet. Lee's formal elegance — the controlled camera, the exquisite framing — is inseparable from his subject: the beautiful, devastating cost of not saying what the body already knows.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• The Wedding Banquet (1993) — gay life in a straight container • Sense and Sensibility (1995) — Austen as Lee's own repression thesis • Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon (2000) — martial arts as female desire • Brokeback Mountain (2005) — the great American love story • Lust, Caution / Se, Jie (2007) — NC-17 WWII espionage and intimacy • Life of Pi (2012) — faith as survival strategy Erotic Art Cinema: Bold, Intimate & Thought-Provoking | Page 7

Shohei Imamura

b. 1926–2006, Tokyo, Japan · Sexuality and animality · Japanese underclass women · Earthy radicalism Imamura declared that his films were about 'the relationship of the lower part of the human body and the lower part of the social structure.' He meant it literally and philosophically. His great films — The Pornographers (1966), The Insect Woman (1963), Intentions of Murder (1964), and the two Palme d'Or winners The Ballad of Narayama (1983) and The Eel (1997) — are all portraits of Japanese women surviving through sexuality, endurance, and animal persistence. Imamura rejected the refinement of Ozu and Mizoguchi in favour of the messy, sexual, funny, and desperate texture of real Japanese working-class life. His cinema is the most fully embodied in Japanese film history.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• The Insect Woman (1963) — fifty years of female survival • Intentions of Murder (1964) — rape survival as black comedy • The Pornographers (1966) — sex industry and the carp • The Ballad of Narayama (1983) — Palme d'Or; elderly ritual death • Black Rain (1989) — Hiroshima survivor • The Eel (1997) — Palme d'Or; redemption and desire Wong Kar-wai b. 1958, Shanghai (works in Hong Kong) · Yearning · Memory · Desire that cannot be acted upon Wong Kar-wai makes films about the space between desire and its fulfillment — and the conviction that the space is where life actually happens. In the Mood for Love (2000) is the definitive example: two people who love each other and never touch, filmed in slow-motion and Nat King Cole, in a Hong Kong that no longer exists. Every Wong Kar-wai film is a film about not having what you want — and the extraordinary beauty of that condition. His collaborations with cinematographer Christopher Doyle created a visual language of romantic longing that influenced an entire generation. 2046 (2004) returns to the same characters and shows desire as a country one can never fully leave.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• Chungking Express (1994) — Hong Kong desire in fast food and expired cans • Fallen Angels (1995) — hitman and the city as unrequited love • Happy Together (1997) — gay Buenos Aires, loneliness abroad • In the Mood for Love (2000) — the great film of unconsummated desire • 2046 (2004) — memory and return • The Grandmaster (2013) — martial arts as elegiac memory Erotic Art Cinema: Bold, Intimate & Thought-Provoking | Page 8

David Cronenberg

b. 1943, Toronto, Canada · Body horror · Technology and flesh · Sexuality as disease and transformation Cronenberg invented a genre — body horror — and used it to examine what modernity does to human sexuality. From Shivers (1975) through Videodrome (1983), The Fly (1986), Naked Lunch (1991), Crash (1996), and A History of Violence (2005), Cronenberg has consistently argued that the flesh is not a stable category — that technology, desire, disease, and imagination all transform it. Crash is his most explicit film: the eroticisation of automobile accidents as a cold, philosophical thought experiment from J.G. Ballard's novel. His son Brandon Cronenberg has continued this tradition. In David's cinema, sex is always a mutation — which is why it always tells the truth.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• Shivers (1975) — apartment building infected with erotic parasite • Videodrome (1983) — television as flesh, flesh as screen • The Fly (1986) — transformation and disease as love story • Naked Lunch (1991) — Burroughs, insects, and the writing body • Crash (1996) — automobile accidents and erotic compulsion • A History of Violence (2005) — American identity and sexual danger • Crimes of the Future (2022) — surgery as performance art

Pedro Almodovar

b. 1949, Calzada de Calatrava, Spain · Female solidarity · Queer identity · Melodrama and desire · Post-Franco Spain Almodovar emerged from the Movida Madrilena — Madrid's explosive post-Franco cultural liberation — and became the great filmmaker of Spanish desire, queer identity, and female experience. His early films (Pepi, Luci, Bom; Labyrinth of Passion; What Have I Done to Deserve This?) were deliberately outrageous, celebrating sexuality in all its forms as political liberation after decades of dictatorship. His mature films — Women on the Verge of a Nervous Breakdown (1988), All About My Mother (1999), Talk to Her (2002), Volver (2006) — refined this into a cinema of extraordinary emotional intelligence and formal beauty. No filmmaker has done more to place women, trans people, and queer experience at the absolute centre of mainstream European cinema.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• Labyrinth of Passion (1982) — post-Franco sexual liberation • Law of Desire (1987) — queer desire as tragedy and comedy • Women on the Verge of a Nervous Breakdown (1988) — international breakthrough • The Flower of My Secret (1995) — identity and melodrama • All About My Mother (1999) — trans identity and motherhood • Talk to Her (2002) — male desire and female unconsciousness • Bad Education (2004) — desire, cinema, and clerical abuse Erotic Art Cinema: Bold, Intimate & Thought-Provoking | Page 9

Jane Campion

b. 1954, Wellington, New Zealand · Female interiority · Power and desire · Gothic feminist sensibility Jane Campion is the only woman to have won the Palme d'Or at Cannes (for The Piano, 1993) and the Academy Award for Best Director (for The Power of the Dog, 2021). Her cinema is defined by an unflinching engagement with female desire, female power, and the violence that greets both. The Piano remains the great feminist erotic film — Holly Hunter's mute Ada communicating through a keyboard and Harvey Keitel's body, in colonial New Zealand, is a portrait of desire as the most radical form of self-expression. Sweetie (1989), An Angel at My Table (1990), Portrait of a Lady (1996), and In the Cut (2003) — a noir about female sexual danger in New York — all share the conviction that female interiority is cinema's most unexplored territory.

ESSENTIAL FILMOGRAPHY:

• Sweetie (1989) — debut; chaotic female desire in suburban Australia • An Angel at My Table (1990) — Janet Frame biography • The Piano (1993) — Palme d'Or; the definitive erotic feminist film • Portrait of a Lady (1996) — James adaptation, female captivity • In the Cut (2003) — Meg Ryan in erotic noir • Top of the Lake (TV, 2013) — New Zealand crime and female trauma • The Power of the Dog (2021) — repressed desire in Montana Erotic Art Cinema: Bold, Intimate & Thought-Provoking | Page 10
