About this collection

A workplace storytelling guide using films and familiar narratives to explain security risks, technical controls, compliance, remediation and operational decisions. It is designed to support team discussion and make abstract security ideas easier to remember.

Suggested approach: Use the material as a discussion and learning aid. Translate analogies into concrete technical decisions, threat scenarios and controls before applying them in a real environment.

YOUR TEAMS — AT A GLANCE

Risk Owns the organisation's risk register and risk appetite. Decides which risks to accept, mitigate, transfer, or avoid. Films in this guide help Risk frame decisions for leadership and board audiences. Technical Compliance Ensures controls meet regulatory and policy requirements — GDPR, ISO 27001,

DORA, NIS2, sector-specific mandates. Films help Compliance explain why controls exist and what failure looks like. Remediation & Operations Executes the fixes. Patches, configurations, hardening, incident containment. Films

help Ops understand the urgency behind the ticket — and the cost of delay. AppSec Governs application security decisions — defines standards, reviews architecture, approves exceptions. Does not do testing. Films help AppSec communicate secure-by-design thinking to development teams. Network Security Owns network architecture, segmentation, perimeter and east-west controls. Films

help Network Security explain why segmentation and monitoring matter. Identity & Access Mgmt Governs who has access to what, under what conditions. MFA, PAM, SSO, access

reviews, lifecycle management. Films help IAM explain why least privilege and access hygiene are critical. Insider Threat Monitors for and responds to threats from within — malicious, negligent, or coerced

insiders. Films help the Insider Threat team explain their work without creating a culture of paranoia.

Seven Samurai Threat Profiling

1954 · Akira Kurosawa

Primary team: Risk Also relevant: Network Security AppSec

CYBER CONCEPT

Understanding attacker motivation, timing, and capability before designing defence

THE ANALOGY

The bandits don't attack randomly. They assess the harvest season, study the village's defences, and time their strike for maximum return. The samurai must think like the bandits to defend effectively — and they discover

the attack comes not where the wall is strongest, but where it was never built.

TELL YOUR TEAM

In your next risk assessment, ask this: if a motivated attacker wanted to cause us the most damage, where would they focus? Not where your controls are strongest — where they're absent, or assumed. The harvest season is

your merger, your product launch, your annual audit window. Attackers study your calendar too.

Key Lesson: Your risk register should reflect attacker opportunity windows, not just technical

vulnerabilities.

Heat Advanced Persistent Threat

1995 · Michael Mann

Primary team: Risk Also relevant: Technical Compliance Insider Threat

CYBER CONCEPT

Long-term, disciplined, professional threat actors — patience and reconnaissance before action

THE ANALOGY

Neil McCauley's crew spends weeks in reconnaissance before touching a target. They maintain strict operational security, have defined roles, leave no forensic trace, and adapt instantly when conditions change.

They are professionals — and they only move when the probability of success is near-certain.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Present this to your Risk committee: an APT is Heat's crew. They are not script kiddies. They are funded, patient, and professional. They have already read your last Annual Report and your job postings. They know your tech stack and your patching cadence. Before they move, they know they can succeed. The question your risk register must answer is: have we made success expensive enough for them to choose a softer target? Key Lesson: Risk appetite decisions must account for professional, patient adversaries — not just

opportunistic ones. —4—

Ocean's Eleven Attack Kill Chain

2001 · Steven Soderbergh

Primary team: Technical Compliance Also relevant: Network Security AppSec

CYBER CONCEPT

Multi-stage coordinated attack — each stage bypassing one specific control

THE ANALOGY

Eleven specialists with eleven roles. The vault is not cracked by brute force — it is opened by layering eleven bypass techniques, each defeating one control. The plan works because each control was assessed individually, not as a system. Compliance said each control was in place. Nobody asked what happened when all eleven

controls were tested simultaneously.

TELL YOUR TEAM

This is your compliance gap. You have a firewall — tick. You have MFA — tick. You have endpoint protection — tick. But Ocean's Eleven doesn't test one control. They chain bypasses across all of them. Your Technical Compliance programme must test controls as a system, not as a checklist. A tick in every box does not equal a

vault that holds. Key Lesson: Compliance against a control list is not the same as assurance that the controls work together.

Test the chain.

The Italian Job Social Engineering + Technical Attack

2003 · F. Gary Gray

Primary team: Technical Compliance Also relevant: Risk Identity & Access Mgmt

CYBER CONCEPT

Human manipulation used alongside technical exploitation — the human is always the easier path

THE ANALOGY

The crew doesn't brute-force the safe. They compromise the guard through trust, create a diversion to absorb security attention, and use the technical tools only once the human layer has been bypassed. The human door is

always tried first.

TELL YOUR TEAM

When your compliance framework says 'staff have completed security awareness training,' ask: does that training address the human attack path or only the technical one? An attacker who wants your credentials will call your helpdesk pretending to be a new starter before they attempt a single technical exploit. Your technical controls protect you from technical attacks. Your people protect you from human ones. Are both equally

mature? Key Lesson: Social engineering targets your people, not your technology. People controls must be as

rigorous as technical controls. —5—

Catch Me If You Can Identity Fraud / Impersonation

2002 · Steven Spielberg

Primary team: Identity & Access Mgmt Also relevant: Technical Compliance Risk

CYBER CONCEPT

Credential theft and impersonation — exploiting the assumption that appearance equals legitimacy

THE ANALOGY

Frank Abagnale doesn't compromise a system. He wears a uniform, speaks with authority, and exploits the human tendency to trust apparent legitimacy. Nobody checks his credentials because he looks like he belongs

— and nobody built a process to verify that he did.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Frank Abagnale is every credential-stuffing attack, every account takeover, every spoofed executive email. He succeeds because verification was never built into the process. For your IAM team: every legitimate-looking login, every plausible-sounding caller, every email from a domain one character different from yours — these are Frank. MFA, privileged access management, and out-of-band verification are the processes Frank cannot

bypass. Key Lesson: The question is never 'does this person look legitimate?' It is: 'have we verified they are who

they claim?' Build the verification process.

Ronin Supply Chain Risk

1998 · John Frankenheimer

Primary team: Risk Also relevant: Technical Compliance AppSec

CYBER CONCEPT

Third-party risk — the unvetted member of the team who compromises the mission

THE ANALOGY

A team assembled from strangers for a high-stakes mission. One member is a mole — not because he was recruited as one, but because he was never properly vetted. The team's trust was extended on the basis of

referral and apparent competence. Nobody asked the hard questions.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Your supply chain is assembled the same way. A SaaS vendor with SOC 2 certification. An open-source library with a thousand stars on GitHub. A contractor with a good CV. Each was added to your environment on the basis of apparent legitimacy. The question your Risk team must put in every vendor assessment: if this third

party is compromised, what do they touch? What can they see? How do we detect it? Key Lesson: Third-party risk assessments must address access scope and detection capability, not just

vendor certifications. —6—

Enemy of the State Data Aggregation Risk

1998 · Tony Scott

Primary team: Risk Also relevant: Technical Compliance Identity & Access Mgmt

CYBER CONCEPT

How individually harmless data points combine into a dangerous asset

THE ANALOGY

Robert Dean's individual data records are harmless. His credit card history, his phone location, his badge access logs — each benign. Aggregated, they become a weapon that destroys his life. The aggregation is the

attack surface, and nobody designed controls around it.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Map this to your data architecture. Your HR system holds employee records. Your badge system holds location data. Your SIEM holds behaviour data. Your ticketing system holds incident history. Individually unremarkable. Accessible together by a single compromised account: a complete map of who is where, who does what, and when your security team is fully occupied. Who has access to all of this simultaneously — and

is that access controlled, logged, and reviewed? Key Lesson: Data aggregation creates risk greater than any single data element. Aggregate access requires

the same controls as privileged access. —7—

Parasite Privilege Escalation / Privilege Creep

2019 · Bong Joon-ho Primary team: Identity & Access Mgmt Also relevant: Technical Compliance Remediation & Operation

CYBER CONCEPT

Gradual, unnoticed privilege escalation through trusted relationships

THE ANALOGY

The Kim family doesn't storm the house. They infiltrate it one role at a time — each member establishing legitimacy before the next enters. By the end they have full access to every room through invitation, not force.

Nobody revoked the earlier access when the next access was granted.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Pull up your access review report and look for privilege creep: the contractor who needed temporary admin rights six months ago and still has them. The developer who had production access for an emergency deployment and was never de-provisioned. The service account created for a project that ended. Each is a Kim family member still in the house. When did you last review access for accounts that shouldn't still have it? Key Lesson: Privilege creep is silent and accumulative. Automated de-provisioning and regular access

reviews are the architectural controls.

The Departed Privileged Account Abuse / Insider

2006 · Martin Scorsese

Primary team: Identity & Access Mgmt Also relevant: Insider Threat Risk

CYBER CONCEPT

Legitimate credentials used to cause maximum harm — the trusted account as the attack vector

THE ANALOGY

Colin Sullivan is a perfectly performing employee. His access looks exactly like a legitimate user's — because it is. The damage he causes is directly proportional to the trust he was given and the access that trust enabled.

Detecting him requires not perimeter defence but behavioural analysis.

TELL YOUR TEAM

The most dangerous accounts in your environment are privileged ones held by humans — system administrators, privileged service accounts, accounts with broad data access. Sullivan-type risk is managed through privileged access management: just-in-time access, session recording, approval workflows for sensitive operations, and behavioural baselines. When a privileged account deviates from its normal pattern — unusual

hours, unusual data volumes, unusual destinations — that is your detection signal. Key Lesson: Privileged access management is the primary control against trusted insider risk. Behaviour,

not identity, is the signal. —8—

Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy Long-term Insider / Persistent Access

2011 · Tomas Alfredson

Primary team: Insider Threat Also relevant: Identity & Access Mgmt Technical Compliance

CYBER CONCEPT

The long-established insider whose access pattern looks normal because it has been normal — until it isn't

THE ANALOGY

The mole has been in place for years. Their access, their relationships, their behaviour — all legitimate. They are detected not through perimeter defence but through patient pattern analysis: looking for the moment

normal behaviour diverges, however slightly.

TELL YOUR TEAM

User and Entity Behaviour Analytics is how you find your mole. The question is not 'is this person authorised?' — they are. The question is: 'is this person accessing data consistent with their role and past behaviour?' When a user who normally reads financial reports from London at 9am starts downloading them at 2am from an unusual location, that deviation is the signal — not the access itself. Has your Insider Threat team defined the

behavioural baselines for your highest-risk accounts? Key Lesson: Insider threat detection requires behavioural baselines and anomaly detection, not just access

controls. Catch Me If You Can Account Takeover / Credential Theft

2002 · Steven Spielberg

Primary team: Identity & Access Mgmt Also relevant: Remediation & Operations Technical Compliance

CYBER CONCEPT

Stolen or borrowed credentials that look indistinguishable from legitimate access

THE ANALOGY

Frank Abagnale's impersonations succeed because the verification process doesn't exist. He looks legitimate. He sounds legitimate. But the system never confirms he is. A stolen credential is Frank: it looks exactly like the

legitimate user — same login, same access pattern — until you look very carefully.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Credential stuffing attacks use valid username-password pairs from previous data breaches to log in to your systems. Your logs show a successful login. Your controls see a legitimate account. The account was never breached here — it was compromised somewhere else and reused. MFA is the single most effective control against credential theft. Every privileged account, every remote access path, every administrative interface

without MFA is a door Frank can open. Key Lesson: MFA is the critical control that breaks credential-based account takeover. No privileged

account should exist without it. —9—

The Firm Vendor Lock-in / Third-Party Leverage

1993 · Sydney Pollack

Primary team: Risk Also relevant: Technical Compliance Identity & Access Mgmt

CYBER CONCEPT

When a vendor or partner relationship creates dangerous dependency and leverage

THE ANALOGY

Mitch McDeere doesn't realise how trapped he is until extracting himself requires more than just leaving. The firm gave him everything — and owns him in return. Getting out requires documented evidence, careful

planning, and a route that doesn't depend on the firm's cooperation.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Before your Risk team signs the next significant vendor contract, model the exit. What data do they hold — in what format? What access do they have — can you revoke it cleanly? What contractual terms prevent you from leaving or from disclosing their practices? The vendor who has your data in a proprietary format, with no data export clause, and direct access to your production environment has leverage over you. Map that leverage

before you sign. Key Lesson: Vendor dependency is a risk. Exit strategy, data portability, and access revocability must be

assessed before engagement. — 10 —

The Big Short Risk Ignored / Systemic Failure

2015 · Adam McKay Primary team: Risk Also relevant: Technical Compliance

CYBER CONCEPT

When known risks are dismissed because acknowledging them is inconvenient

THE ANALOGY

The ratings agencies knew the mortgage-backed securities were fragile. The banks knew. The regulators knew — or should have. The risk was visible in the data. It was ignored because acting on it was commercially

inconvenient, and because everyone assumed someone else would stop it first.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Bring this to your Risk committee when a known vulnerability sits unpatched for six months because the business case for remediation hasn't been approved. The risk is on the register. The register was reviewed. Nobody actioned it because the cost of action exceeded the accepted discomfort. Until the breach. The Big Short question for every risk item: are we not acting because the risk is genuinely low, or because acting is

inconvenient and we're hoping the problem resolves itself? Key Lesson: A risk on the register that remains unmitigated is a decision — make it explicitly, not by

default.

Margin Call Risk Escalation / Decision Under

2011 · J.C. Chandor Uncertainty

Primary team: Risk Also relevant: Technical Compliance Remediation & Operation

CYBER CONCEPT

Making high-stakes decisions at speed with incomplete information — and who has accountability

THE ANALOGY

A junior analyst discovers that the firm's positions exceed the entire market's capacity to absorb them. The discovery escalates through the night. At each level, the question changes: can we fix it, or must we manage the

fall? Who authorises the decision? Who bears the accountability?

TELL YOUR TEAM

This is your critical vulnerability escalation scenario. A compliance analyst finds a systemic control failure at 6pm on a Friday. It escalates: team lead, CISO, CRO, board. At each level: what do we know? What is the exposure? What are the options? Who decides? The quality of your escalation path — who gets called, in what order, with what decision authority — determines whether you manage the crisis or the crisis manages you.

When did your team last test that path? Key Lesson: Risk escalation paths must be defined, documented, and practised before the crisis that

requires them. — 11 —

Moneyball Data-Driven Risk Decisions

2011 · Bennett Miller

Primary team: Risk Also relevant: Technical Compliance AppSec

CYBER CONCEPT

Using data and evidence to make better risk decisions against conventional wisdom

THE ANALOGY

Billy Beane doesn't trust the scouts' intuition — he trusts the statistics. The scouts tell him which players look like good players. The data tells him which players produce outcomes. The gap between appearance and

evidence is where the value is hidden.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Your security risk decisions are often based on intuition and convention: 'we've always done it this way,' 'this control feels right,' 'this vendor is well-known.' The Moneyball question is: what does the data say? Which controls are actually reducing incidents? Which risk items on your register have never materialised? Which

investments have the highest evidence-based return? Metrics-driven risk management outperforms

intuition-driven risk management — over time. Key Lesson: Risk investment decisions should be driven by evidence of effectiveness, not convention or

vendor marketing. A Few Good Men Security Culture / Authority &

1992 · Rob Reiner Complicity

Primary team: Risk Also relevant: Technical Compliance Insider Threat

CYBER CONCEPT

When organisational culture makes security failures possible through unquestioned authority

THE ANALOGY

Nobody questioned the order. Questioning was not the culture. The entire chain of command was complicit — not because they agreed, but because the culture made objection impossible. The result was a preventable harm

that destroyed careers and a life.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Security incidents are often enabled by culture, not technology. Nobody questioned why the CEO needed the wire transfer done without the usual approval process. Nobody asked why this integration needed direct database access. 'My manager told me to' is not a security control. If your organisation doesn't have safe channels for 'this doesn't look right,' you don't have a security culture — you have a security policy. What is the

actual lived experience of someone who raises a security concern? Key Lesson: Psychological safety to raise security concerns is a control. Build it deliberately or your

policies will be paper. — 12 —

Chinatown Root Cause Analysis / Systemic Risk

1974 · Roman Polanski

Primary team: Risk Also relevant: Technical Compliance AppSec

CYBER CONCEPT

The presenting problem is never the real problem — systemic analysis is required

THE ANALOGY

Jake Gittes thinks he understands the crime at every stage. He's wrong at every level. The real problem is not the water theft — it's the power structure that makes the water theft possible and protects it. Every surface

finding points to a deeper, more systemic truth.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Post-incident reviews that conclude 'an employee clicked a phishing link' are Chinatown. That is the surface. The real questions: why did the phishing email reach the inbox? Why did the user have the privileges that made the click dangerous? Why was there no MFA on the account? Why wasn't lateral movement detected? Each answer reveals a deeper control gap. Root cause analysis must go past the immediate technical finding to the

systemic conditions that made it possible. Key Lesson: Root cause analysis must reach systemic causes, not just proximate ones. The fix must match

the real problem. — 13 —

The China Syndrome Vulnerability Suppression /

1979 · James Bridges Non-disclosure Primary team: Technical Compliance Also relevant: Risk AppSec

CYBER CONCEPT

Known vulnerabilities suppressed rather than disclosed — the cover-up is always worse

THE ANALOGY

The nuclear plant has a structural flaw. The people who know it refuse to disclose — for business reasons, for reputation, for fear of the investigation that would follow. When the truth can no longer be suppressed, the

consequences are catastrophic and permanent.

TELL YOUR TEAM

When your compliance team finds a control failure three weeks before an audit or a product launch, this is the moment. The pressure to delay disclosure — to fix it quietly before anyone notices — is The China Syndrome temptation. But the regulatory frameworks your compliance function operates under — GDPR, DORA, NIS2 — have mandatory disclosure timelines precisely because covered-up failures always cause more harm than

disclosed ones. Build a culture where finding a problem is rewarded, not concealed. Key Lesson: Timely, accurate disclosure of control failures is both a regulatory requirement and a

risk-reduction strategy.

Brazil Security Process Dysfunction /

1985 · Terry Gilliam Over-Compliance

Primary team: Technical Compliance Also relevant: Remediation & Operations AppSec

CYBER CONCEPT

When process complexity undermines the security it was designed to provide

THE ANALOGY

The Ministry of Information's bureaucracy is so complex that it cannot respond to actual threats. The forms, approvals, and procedures consume all available capacity. The system is compliant with itself — and

simultaneously unable to function.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Audit your own compliance processes from the user's perspective. If reporting a phishing email requires a six-field form and a 72-hour response SLA, people stop reporting. If patching a critical vulnerability requires three change management approvals, the window for exploitation stays open. If your security awareness training is a 47-slide deck with a pass/fail quiz, nobody learns. Compliance processes that are too burdensome are exploited through workarounds — and workarounds are gaps. Simplify what you measure. Measure what

actually reduces risk. Key Lesson: Compliance processes must be frictionless enough to be followed consistently. Burdensome

controls create the workarounds that defeat them. — 14 —

Spotlight Audit, Evidence & Forensic Readiness

2015 · Tom McCarthy Primary team: Technical Compliance Also relevant: Remediation & Operations Insider Threat

CYBER CONCEPT

Evidence-based investigation — the records were always there, nobody read them systematically

THE ANALOGY

The Spotlight team doesn't find new evidence. They find patterns in evidence that was always there — court records, employment files, archdiocesan directories. The institution was protected not by absence of evidence

but by absence of anyone who looked at the evidence systematically.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Forensic readiness is your Spotlight preparation. After a breach, the investigation requires logs — authentication logs, network flow data, endpoint telemetry, application logs. The evidence must exist, be complete, be tamper-protected, and be retained for long enough to support the investigation. The worst discovery in a post-incident investigation is: we didn't have the logs. Has your compliance function verified that

your logging coverage matches your investigation requirements? Key Lesson: Forensic readiness means having the right logs, retained for the right duration, protected from

tampering — before you need them.

Erin Brockovich Regulatory Breach / Accountability

2000 · Steven Soderbergh

Primary team: Technical Compliance Also relevant: Risk

CYBER CONCEPT

The gap between what the organisation knew and what it disclosed — and the consequences

THE ANALOGY

Pacific Gas & Electric knew about the contamination. The harm was documented internally. The decision was made not to disclose. When the truth emerged, the liability dwarfed what remediation would have cost. The

cover was more expensive than the crisis.

TELL YOUR TEAM

Map this to your breach notification obligations. Under GDPR Article 33, you have 72 hours from becoming aware of a personal data breach to notify the supervisory authority. Under NIS2 and DORA, similar timelines apply to significant incidents. The temptation to investigate fully before disclosing — to 'be certain' before notifying — is the Erin Brockovich temptation. Regulators understand uncertainty in early notification. They do not forgive deliberate delay. Prepare your breach notification playbook now, not in hour two of an active

incident. Key Lesson: Breach notification obligations have tight timelines. Prepare the process before the incident,

not during it. — 15 —
