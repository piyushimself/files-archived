About This Collection

Art history is usually taught through masterpieces — the what. This guide is organised around the who and the how: the daily life, the obsessive habits, the personal eccentricities, and the specific qualities of vision that separated these

25 artists from everyone else who held a brush, a camera, or a spray can. Every great artist has a practice — a set of working rituals, physical habits, and psychological strategies that made their particular kind of seeing possible. Van Gogh's breakneck speed and near-starvation were not incidental to his work; they were part of how it was made. Vermeer's extraordinary slowness — two or three paintings a year — was not inefficiency but the condition of his precision. Hokusai's 90 changes of address were not restlessness but a

systematic attempt to see new things. Each profile includes: a biography focused on the facts that illuminate the work; a selection of major works with their current locations; observed habits and working methods from contemporary accounts and letters; and a statement of what specifically distinguished this artist from every other artist of their time — the precise contribution they made

that no one else made. The artists are arranged in a loose chronological order within the guide, but each is a complete portrait that stands

alone. Begin anywhere. Masters of Art: Lives, Works & Habits · The Definitive Artist Reference · Page 2

01Leonardo da Vinci RENAISSANCE 1452–1519 · Vinci, Republic of Florence (Italy) · Painting, drawing, sculpture, science, anatomy, engineering

BIOGRAPHY: The son of a Florentine notary and a peasant woman, Leonardo was apprenticed at 14 to Andrea del Verrocchio and never stopped learning. He kept notebooks — over 7,000 pages survive — filled with mirror-written observations on anatomy, flight, water, geology, architecture, and weaponry. He dissected at least 30 human corpses to understand musculature, and his anatomical drawings were so accurate they were not surpassed for centuries. He left most of his paintings unfinished — perfectionism and an insatiable curiosity that always found the next thing more interesting than completing the last.

FAMOUS WORKS:

  • Mona Lisa (c.1503–19) — Louvre, Paris
  • The Last Supper (1495–98) — Santa Maria delle Grazie, Milan
  • Vitruvian Man (c.1490) — Gallerie dell'Accademia, Venice
  • Lady with an Ermine (c.1489–91) — Czartoryski Museum, Krakow
  • The Virgin of the Rocks (1483–86) — Louvre, Paris
  • Salvator Mundi (c.1500) — Private collection (sold $450M, 2017)

HABITS, RITUALS & WORKING METHODS:

  • Wrote in mirror script, right to left, in his notebooks — possibly to prevent copying, possibly left-handedness, possibly habit.
  • Slept in multiple short cycles (polyphasic sleep) — allegedly 20-minute naps every 4 hours rather than one long sleep.
  • Carried his notebook everywhere and sketched obsessively — he would follow interesting strangers through the streets of

Florence for hours to capture their expressions.

  • Was a vegetarian and reportedly bought caged birds at markets solely to release them.
  • Worked on the Mona Lisa for 16 years and may never have considered it finished — he carried it with him until his death.
  • Procrastinated spectacularly — he was once paid in advance for a painting that he never delivered. His patron waited decades.

WHAT MADE THEM DIFFERENT: The breadth — no other individual in history has contributed meaningfully to as many different fields simultaneously. His sfumato technique (the soft, smoky boundary between tones) transformed painting's relationship to light. His scientific curiosity was not separate from his art but the same faculty — both were about seeing more accurately than anyone had before.

"Learning never exhausts the mind." 02Michelangelo Buonarroti RENAISSANCE 1475–1564 · Caprese, Republic of Florence (Italy) · Sculpture, painting, architecture, poetry

BIOGRAPHY: Michelangelo considered himself primarily a sculptor — 'I am a sculptor,' he would insist to anyone who asked him to paint. Pope Julius II did not care and commissioned the Sistine Chapel ceiling regardless. He painted it largely alone, lying on scaffolding 20 metres above the floor, over four years, suffering chronic neck and back pain that lasted the rest of his life. He lived to 88 — extraordinarily old for the era — and was working on his final Pieta three days before his death. He wrote over 300 surviving

poems, many of them about art, death, and unrequited love for young men. FAMOUS WORKS:

  • David (1501–04) — Galleria dell'Accademia, Florence

  • Pieta (1498–99) — St. Peter's Basilica, Vatican
  • Sistine Chapel Ceiling (1508–12) — Vatican
  • The Creation of Adam (detail, 1508–12) — Vatican
  • Moses (c.1513–15) — San Pietro in Vincoli, Rome

HABITS, RITUALS & WORKING METHODS:

  • Wore his boots for such extended periods that when he finally removed them, the skin came off with them.
  • Slept on the floor of his studio, fully clothed, to maximise work hours.
  • Ate bread and water almost exclusively while working on major commissions — he considered eating a distraction.
  • Drew so obsessively that he destroyed thousands of preparatory drawings before his death to prevent anyone from seeing how

much work went into his apparent effortlessness.

  • Had an explosive temper — he broke a model's nose in a fight (the model was Pietro Torrigiano) and was beaten in return.
  • Believed he received visions of his figures already inside the marble — his famous quote about sculpture as liberating the figure

from the stone is a genuine description of his creative process. WHAT MADE THEM DIFFERENT: The physical and intellectual scale. No other artist has produced supreme achievements in sculpture (David, Pieta), painting (Sistine ceiling), and architecture (St. Peter's dome) simultaneously. His figures possess a psychological intensity — terribilita — that no previous artist had achieved. His human bodies are ideals that somehow also look like they hurt. "The greater danger for most of us lies not in setting our aim too high and falling short, but in setting our aim too

low and achieving our mark." 03Rembrandt van Rijn BAROQUE 1606–1669 · Leiden, Dutch Republic (Netherlands) · Oil painting, etching, drawing BIOGRAPHY: Rembrandt painted himself over 90 times across his career — more than any other artist in history until the age of the selfie. He was the son of a miller, became the most fashionable portrait painter in Amsterdam in his 20s, and went spectacularly bankrupt at 50 when his spending on art, antiquities, and studio props outpaced even his enormous commissions. He moved to a smaller house, kept working, and produced his greatest paintings in poverty. The Night Watch, contrary to popular legend, was not

criticised by its commissioners — it simply changed portraiture forever. FAMOUS WORKS:

  • The Night Watch (1642) — Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam
  • Self-Portrait with Two Circles (c.1665–69) — Kenwood House, London
  • The Anatomy Lesson of Dr. Nicolaes Tulp (1632) — Mauritshuis, The Hague
  • Return of the Prodigal Son (c.1668) — Hermitage, St. Petersburg
  • Bathsheba at Her Bath (1654) — Louvre, Paris
  • Self-Portrait (1659) — National Gallery of Art, Washington D.C.

HABITS, RITUALS & WORKING METHODS:

  • Collected obsessively — his bankruptcy inventory listed armour, plaster casts, natural curiosities, Asian art, and thousands of

prints that he used as compositional references. Masters of Art: Lives, Works & Habits · The Definitive Artist Reference · Page 4

  • Mixed his own paints with unusual ingredients — lead white, smalt, natural resins — creating textures that still puzzle

conservators.

  • Drew from life constantly but used his collection of props and costumes to construct imaginary scenes.
  • His studio was famously chaotic — students were warned not to touch anything. Objects and paintings covered every surface.
  • Painted in thin glazes that he would wait to dry before adding the next layer — the Prodigal Son's robe was built up in dozens of

translucent layers.

  • Rarely left Amsterdam in his adult life — the entire world came to sit for him.

WHAT MADE THEM DIFFERENT: Chiaroscuro — the use of extreme contrast between light and dark — in service of psychological truth. His portraits are not flattering; they are honest. His faces look tired, worried, old, real. His self-portraits constitute the most sustained visual autobiography in art history: you watch him age, prosperous and then bankrupt, young and then ancient. "Try to put well in practice what you already know; in so doing, you will, in good time, discover the hidden things

you now inquire about." 04Vincent van Gogh POST-IMPRESSIONISM 1853–1890 · Zundert, Netherlands · Oil painting, drawing, watercolour BIOGRAPHY: Van Gogh only began painting seriously at 27, having failed as an art dealer, a teacher, and a missionary. He produced over 2,100 artworks in roughly 10 years of working life — an average of nearly one painting every two days — before shooting himself at

  1. He sold only one painting during his lifetime. He wrote over 800 letters to his brother Theo, which together form one of the

greatest self-analyses of an artist's mind ever written. His mental illness — possibly bipolar disorder, possibly temporal lobe

epilepsy, possibly both — was inseparable from his vision and his productivity. FAMOUS WORKS:

  • The Starry Night (1889) — MoMA, New York
  • Sunflowers series (1888–89) — various museums
  • Self-Portrait with Bandaged Ear (1889) — Courtauld Gallery, London
  • Bedroom in Arles (1888) — Art Institute of Chicago
  • Wheatfield with Crows (1890) — Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam
  • Irises (1889) — Getty Museum, Los Angeles

HABITS, RITUALS & WORKING METHODS:

  • Painted with extraordinary physical speed — he would often complete a painting in a single day, working in fierce bursts.
  • Ate almost nothing when working — he would go days consuming only coffee and bread, spending his allowance from Theo on

paint.

  • Ate paint directly — he was seen chewing on his paint tubes, possibly from hunger, possibly as a compulsive act.
  • Wrote letters of extraordinary literary intelligence — his letters to Theo describe colour theory, artistic philosophy, and his

mental state with complete clarity.

  • Painted outdoors in all weather, strapping his easel to stakes in the ground when the mistral wind blew.
  • Cut off part of his ear following a breakdown and gave it to a woman at a local brothel. He then painted himself the next day.

WHAT MADE THEM DIFFERENT: The visible brushstroke as emotion. Van Gogh made paint application the language of feeling — his swirling skies and writhing cypresses are not description but translation of interior experience into visible form. He used colour not naturalistically but

"I put my heart and my soul into my work, and have lost my mind in the process." 05Pablo Picasso CUBISM / MODERN 1881–1973 · Malaga, Spain (worked in Paris) · Painting, sculpture, ceramics, collage, printmaking

BIOGRAPHY: Picasso produced an estimated 20,000 works across 80 years of working life. His father was an art teacher who reportedly handed his son his palette and brushes when Pablo was 13, declaring he would never paint again because his son had surpassed him. Whether true or apocryphal, Picasso repeated the story often. He co-invented Cubism with Braque — the fragmentation of perspective that permanently altered how Western art represented the world. Guernica, painted in six weeks in response to the

Nazi bombing of a Basque town, is the most politically powerful painting of the 20th century.

FAMOUS WORKS:

  • Guernica (1937) — Museo Reina Sofia, Madrid
  • Les Demoiselles d'Avignon (1907) — MoMA, New York
  • Girl Before a Mirror (1932) — MoMA, New York
  • The Weeping Woman (1937) — Tate Modern, London
  • The Old Guitarist (1903–04) — Art Institute of Chicago
  • Ma Jolie (1911–12) — MoMA, New York

HABITS, RITUALS & WORKING METHODS:

  • Woke late — he rarely began working before noon and worked until well past midnight.
  • Was superstitious about haircuts and kept his cut hair in envelopes, believing it contained part of his vital energy.
  • Never threw anything away — his studios were so full of objects, paintings, and detritus that assistants could barely move.
  • Changed his style radically with each major relationship — his Blue Period, Rose Period, Cubist Period, and Neoclassical

Period each correspond to different women in his life.

  • Disliked touching or being touched by strangers — in meetings he would hold an object in his hand so people couldn't grab it.
  • Had a photographic memory — he could see a face once and reproduce it precisely years later.

WHAT MADE THEM DIFFERENT: The refusal to stop reinventing. Picasso mastered academic painting before destroying it. He could draw with absolute classical precision — his early academic work is jaw-dropping — and then chose to abandon that precision for something truer. Cubism was not inability but a philosophical decision: the way we look at things is not the way they are. He applied that decision to his

entire life. "Every act of creation is first an act of destruction." 06Frida Kahlo SURREALISM / MODERN 1907–1954 · Coyoacan, Mexico · Oil painting, self-portrait BIOGRAPHY: Masters of Art: Lives, Works & Habits · The Definitive Artist Reference · Page 6

Kahlo contracted polio at 6, which left her right leg thinner than her left, and survived a catastrophic bus accident at 18 that fractured her spine, collarbone, ribs, and pelvis and impaled her with a steel handrail. She spent most of her remaining life in a medical corset and underwent 35 surgeries. She began painting seriously while immobilised in bed, using a specially constructed easel and a mirror mounted above her. Her self-portraits — about a third of her total output — are not narcissism but the only subject constantly available to her. She married and twice divorced the muralist Diego Rivera. She died at 47, possibly by suicide,

possibly pneumonia. FAMOUS WORKS:

  • The Two Fridas (1939) — Museo de Arte Moderno, Mexico City
  • Self-Portrait with Thorn Necklace and Hummingbird (1940) — Blanton Museum
  • The Broken Column (1944) — Museo Dolores Olmedo, Mexico City
  • What the Water Gave Me (1938) — Private collection
  • My Birth (1932) — Private collection
  • Self-Portrait with Cropped Hair (1940) — MoMA, New York

HABITS, RITUALS & WORKING METHODS:

  • Painted in bed during her multiple periods of immobilisation, using a special overhead easel and mirror constructed by her

mother.

  • Wore traditional Tehuana costume deliberately — the elaborate skirts, braids, and flowers were both personal aesthetic and

political statement of Mexican indigenous identity.

  • Kept a diary of extraordinary visual creativity in her final decade — a mixture of prose, drawings, and collage that was not

published until 1995.

  • Smoked, drank tequila, and threw raucous parties — her home, the Blue House (Casa Azul), was the social centre of Mexico

City's intellectual life.

  • Named her pet deer Granizo, her eagle Gertrudis Caca Blanca, and treated all her animals as intimates, including them in her

paintings.

  • Rejected the Surrealist label with characteristic bluntness: 'I never painted dreams. I painted my own reality.'

WHAT MADE THEM DIFFERENT: The transformation of physical suffering into visual language. Her paintings are medical documents and political manifestos simultaneously. She invented a symbolic vocabulary — bleeding hearts, broken columns, hummingbirds, Tehuana costume, the two Fridas — that was entirely personal and became instantly universal. She made the female body the site of historical and

political meaning before feminist art had a name. "I paint my own reality. The only thing I know is that I paint because I need to, and I paint whatever passes through

my head without any other consideration." 07Claude Monet IMPRESSIONISM 1840–1926 · Paris, France (lived in Giverny) · Oil painting (en plein air) BIOGRAPHY: Monet gave Impressionism its name — from a critic who meant it as an insult. He spent the second half of his life in Giverny designing a garden specifically to paint. The water lily pond was a constructed subject — he diverted a river, planted the lilies, built the Japanese bridge, and painted it for 30 years in every light and season. In his final decade he was nearly blind from cataracts, painting enormous canvases from memory and feel. His 8 Water Lilies panels in the Orangerie, which he arranged as a complete circular environment, are the first fully immersive painting installation in art history.

FAMOUS WORKS:

  • Water Lilies series (1896–1926) — Orangerie, Paris; MoMA, New York; many others
  • Impression, Sunrise (1872) — Musee Marmottan Monet, Paris
  • Haystacks series (1890–91) — various museums
  • Rouen Cathedral series (1892–94) — various museums
  • Woman with a Parasol (1875) — National Gallery of Art, Washington
  • Bridge over a Pond of Water Lilies (1899) — Metropolitan Museum, New York

HABITS, RITUALS & WORKING METHODS:

  • Rose at 4 or 5 AM every morning to check the light and weather conditions before deciding which canvas to work on that day.
  • Worked on dozens of canvases simultaneously — he would carry multiple canvases outdoors, switching between them as the

light changed every 20-30 minutes.

  • Planted and maintained his garden himself, directing gardeners with the precision of a compositional decision.
  • Ate the same lunch every day — he was an obsessive cook and kept detailed records of his recipes and menus.
  • Wore linen dusters while working and was known for his enormous straw hat and bushy beard, which became as iconic as his

paintings.

  • Continued painting after nearly losing his sight, identifying paint tubes by their labels rather than colour, working in what he

later described as a sort of desperate joy. WHAT MADE THEM DIFFERENT: The insistence that light, not the objects it falls on, is the true subject of painting. Monet saw that a haystack at noon and a haystack at sunset are different objects — the series format was his solution. The Water Lilies are not paintings of water lilies but of the surface of light reflected in still water, which is something entirely different and entirely original.

"I must have flowers, always, and always." 08Salvador Dali SURREALISM 1904–1989 · Figueres, Catalonia, Spain · Painting, sculpture, film, performance BIOGRAPHY: Dali was expelled from the Madrid School of Fine Arts (twice) and the Surrealist movement (by Andre Breton, who renamed him 'Avida Dollars' — an anagram of Salvador Dali — for his commercial ambitions). He didn't care. He gave press conferences with an anteater on a leash. He collaborated with Alfred Hitchcock on the dream sequence in Spellbound and with Walt Disney on a short film never completed in his lifetime. He was deeply in love with his wife Gala — a Russian woman married to someone else when they met — and dedicated every major work to her. He died in a castle he had built for her, alone, because she had died

six years earlier. FAMOUS WORKS:

  • The Persistence of Memory (1931) — MoMA, New York
  • The Elephants (1948) — Museo Reina Sofia, Madrid
  • Christ of Saint John of the Cross (1951) — Kelvingrove Art Gallery, Glasgow
  • Lobster Phone (1936) — Tate Modern, London
  • Dream Caused by the Flight of a Bee (1944) — Thyssen Museum, Madrid
  • The Hallucinogenic Toreador (1969–70) — Salvador Dali Museum, St. Petersburg FL

  • Used a technique he called 'slumber with a key' — he would fall asleep in a chair holding a heavy key over a metal plate. The

moment he lost consciousness and dropped the key, he would wake and immediately sketch the hypnagogic imagery.

  • Carried an elaborate cane with an ornate golden pommel as his signature prop and used it theatrically in public.
  • Made detailed calculations — he claimed The Persistence of Memory came to him in 20 minutes but had spent weeks studying

the previous work it built on.

  • Was terrified of grasshoppers — a childhood trauma that appeared in multiple paintings.
  • Shaved his body hair before important interviews, believing it removed mental noise.
  • Charged astronomical fees for his signature — literally just his signature on blank pages — as a revenue stream, leading to

concerns that his output includes thousands of 'authenticated' forgeries. WHAT MADE THEM DIFFERENT: The application of absolute technical mastery to completely irrational content. Dali painted soft watches and burning giraffes with the detailed precision of a Flemish Old Master. The combination — hyper-real execution of impossible subject matter — created

a visual vocabulary for the unconscious that has never been surpassed for memorability. "Have no fear of perfection — you'll never reach it." 09Katsushika Hokusai UKIYO-E 1760–1849 · Edo (Tokyo), Japan · Woodblock print, painting, illustrated books BIOGRAPHY: Hokusai changed his name 30 times during his life, as was Japanese custom when an artist believed they had reached a new level of mastery. He was working at the level of genius by his 30s, producing thousands of prints and illustrated books, but wrote in his preface to One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji: 'All I have produced before the age of 70 is not worth taking into account. At 73 I began to grasp the structure of birds and beasts, insects and fish, and the way plants grow. If I go on trying, I will surely understand them still better by the time I am 86, so that by 90 I will have penetrated to their essential nature. At 100, I may have a divine understanding of them, and at 110, every dot and every stroke will be as if alive.' He died at 89, reportedly saying: 'If

heaven would give me just five more years.' FAMOUS WORKS:

  • The Great Wave off Kanagawa (c.1831) — Metropolitan Museum, New York
  • Fine Wind, Clear Morning (Red Fuji) (c.1830–32) — various collections
  • Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji series (c.1830–33)
  • Dream of the Fisherman's Wife (1814) — various collections
  • A Tour of the Waterfalls of the Provinces series (c.1832)
  • Manga (1814–78) — 15 volumes of sketches

HABITS, RITUALS & WORKING METHODS:

  • Moved house over 90 times during his life — when a studio became too cluttered or simply too familiar, he moved rather than

cleaned.

  • Worked at a low desk on the floor, sitting cross-legged, often for 16-18 hours at a stretch.
  • Considered cleanliness irrelevant — he was legendarily filthy, reportedly never bathing, and was served by his daughter Oei

who managed the household and assisted in his studio.

  • His daughter Oei was arguably as talented as him — she painted his figures for years and her own work is only now being

separated from his and recognised. Masters of Art: Lives, Works & Habits · The Definitive Artist Reference · Page 9

  • Made live demonstrations — he once painted a portrait of Daruma (Bodhidharma) using a broom dipped in ink, working

outdoors in front of a crowd.

  • Never stopped improving his technique — he was studying new subjects and new methods in his late 80s.

WHAT MADE THEM DIFFERENT: The democratisation of beauty. Ukiyo-e prints were mass-produced and inexpensive — Hokusai made sublime landscapes available to ordinary people. The Great Wave is the most reproduced image in the history of art for a reason: it captures the relationship between human scale and natural force with an elegance that has never been repeated. His influence on Van Gogh,

Monet, Toulouse-Lautrec, and an entire generation of European artists is incalculable. "From the age of 6 I had a mania for drawing. By 50 I had published an infinity of designs. Whatever I drew after

70 years was truly worthy." 10Georgia O'Keeffe MODERN / AMERICAN MODERNISM 1887–1986 · Sun Prairie, Wisconsin, USA (lived in New Mexico) · Oil painting, watercolour BIOGRAPHY: O'Keeffe lived to 98, spending her most productive decades in New Mexico, where the Pedernal mesa and the desert light gave her subjects for 50 years. She had begun a relationship with the photographer Alfred Stieglitz in New York, who exhibited her work and promoted her obsessively — and whose death in 1946 sent her permanently to New Mexico. She drove herself across the desert in a black Model A Ford, hiked alone in canyons at 70, and in her final years, nearly blind, worked with a younger assistant she had brought to live with her. She described the enormous close-up flower paintings — which everyone saw as erotic — as simply: 'Nobody sees a flower, really; it is so small — we haven't time, and to see takes time, like to have a friend takes

time.' FAMOUS WORKS:

  • Jimson Weed/White Flower No. 1 (1932) — Crystal Bridges Museum
  • Sky Above Clouds IV (1965) — Art Institute of Chicago
  • Ram's Head White Hollyhock and Little Hills (1935) — Brooklyn Museum
  • Black Iris III (1926) — Metropolitan Museum, New York
  • Cow's Skull: Red, White, and Blue (1931) — Metropolitan Museum
  • New York Street with Moon (1925) — Carmen Thyssen Museum, Madrid

HABITS, RITUALS & WORKING METHODS:

  • Rose before dawn every day to drive into the desert before the light changed, often sleeping in her car overnight near a painting

location.

  • Wore only black and white — she simplified her wardrobe so completely that choosing clothes required no mental energy.
  • Kept her studio and home in Ghost Ranch almost completely bare — white walls, a few bones, a skull, a Zen-like asceticism in

contrast to her enormous paintings.

  • Collected bones from the desert floor — cattle skulls, pelvis bones, vertebrae — and used them as studies for years.
  • Refused to explain her flower paintings. When pressed on their sexual symbolism, she denied it completely and permanently.
  • Practiced early yoga decades before it became mainstream in America, having encountered it through Stieglitz's circle.

WHAT MADE THEM DIFFERENT: The extreme close-up as a philosophical act. O'Keeffe took a flower or a bone or the aperture of a pelvis and enlarged it until it became landscape. She invented a visual language that was simultaneously abstract and concrete, simultaneously American and universal. The New Mexico desert gave her a subject as endlessly varied as Monet's water lily pond.

"I've been absolutely terrified every moment of my life, and I've never let it keep me from doing a single thing I

wanted to do." 11Caravaggio (Michelangelo Merisi) BAROQUE 1571–1610 · Milan, Duchy of Milan (Italy) · Oil painting BIOGRAPHY: Caravaggio was a murderer, a brawler, a fugitive, and the most influential painter of the 17th century. He killed a man in Rome in 1606 — possibly in a fight over a tennis match, possibly a premeditated revenge — and spent the last four years of his life as a wanted man, moving between Naples, Malta, Sicily, and back toward Rome, where he had been granted a partial pardon. He died of a fever at 38, possibly 39, on a beach in Porto Ercole, while trying to reach Rome. He transformed European painting with a technique of using real Roman street people as models for biblical figures and lighting scenes as if from a single candle in

darkness. FAMOUS WORKS:

  • The Calling of Saint Matthew (1599–1600) — San Luigi dei Francesi, Rome
  • Judith Beheading Holofernes (1598–99) — Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica, Rome
  • The Death of the Virgin (1601–06) — Louvre, Paris
  • David with the Head of Goliath (1610) — Borghese Gallery, Rome
  • Supper at Emmaus (1601) — National Gallery, London
  • The Flagellation of Christ (1607) — Capodimonte Museum, Naples

HABITS, RITUALS & WORKING METHODS:

  • Wore the same outfit for weeks until it fell apart — he was repeatedly described as dressed in rags despite being one of the

highest-paid painters in Rome.

  • Slept with his sword — always armed, always dangerous, always paranoid.
  • Used real corpses as models — his Death of the Virgin, which was rejected by its first buyers as scandalous, allegedly used the

body of a drowned prostitute.

  • Painted without preparatory drawings — he worked directly onto the canvas, which was revolutionary and considered

improper by academic standards.

  • Kept a dog called Crow as his constant companion through his years of flight.
  • May have used a system of mirrors and lenses to project images onto his canvas as compositional guides — art historians

continue to debate this. WHAT MADE THEM DIFFERENT: Tenebrism — extreme chiaroscuro where figures emerge from near-total darkness into a single, raking light source. Before Caravaggio, biblical scenes were painted in idealised, heavenly light. He put Christ in a tavern, lit by a candle, surrounded by ordinary Romans with dirty fingernails. This was radical and revolutionary and permanently changed what painting could show. "As the flame does not need to defend its own light, so the truth does not need the defense of authority."

12Henri Matisse MODERN / FAUVISM 1869–1954 · Le Cateau-Cambresis, France · Painting, sculpture, cut-outs, ceramics BIOGRAPHY: Matisse began painting at 20 while recovering from appendicitis when his mother gave him a paint set. He described it as a paradise he had never imagined. He trained formally, discovered Cezanne at 28, and spent the next 60 years pursuing what he called 'an art of balance, of purity and serenity.' In his final decade, bedridden after major surgery, he invented the cut-out: scissors cutting through paper painted with brilliant gouache, assembled by assistants who followed his directions from his bed.

He called it 'drawing with scissors.' He was working on the last cut-out when he died at 84.

FAMOUS WORKS:

  • The Dance (1909–10) — Hermitage, St. Petersburg
  • Woman with a Hat (1905) — San Francisco Museum of Modern Art
  • Blue Nudes series (1952) — various museums
  • The Red Studio (1911) — MoMA, New York

HABITS, RITUALS & WORKING METHODS:

  • Painted in Armani suits — he was photographed by Annie Leibovitz barefoot in an Armani jacket in front of a canvas, and the

image became iconic.

  • Worked to jazz and hip-hop, keeping the music playing continuously in his studio.
  • Covered his canvases with text — words crossed out, repeated, isolated in boxes — treating language as visual material with

the same freedom as paint.

  • Collected jazz records obsessively — Charlie Parker, Miles Davis, and Dizzy Gillespie were as important to his work as any

visual artist. Masters of Art: Lives, Works & Habits · The Definitive Artist Reference · Page 22
